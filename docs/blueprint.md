# Blueprint

What must be true of vtt-platform, whatever it is built from. Replace every
language, store, transport and library it uses today and each principle below
still holds.

The system has one set of principles that runs through all of it, and three
areas:

- **The board engine** — how things happen on the board: who is connected,
  what is seen, how one moves.
- **The rules engine** — how the system carries out what happens, by reading a
  ruleset.
- **The interfaces** — the campaign's and the rules', the only ways in.

The principles are absolutes. A requirement that does not fit one is met by
naming the principle it breaks, what breaking it costs and who decided, never
by an exception written here in advance.

Words used here as the system's own: a *participant* is anyone at the table —
the DM, a player, a spectator or an agent; the *record* is what happened, in
order, and an *entry* is one thing in it; a *ruleset* is a game system as
data; a *sheet* is what a campaign holds about one character or creature.

## The whole system

### 1. Every participant acts through the same interface.

**Why.** An action only one kind of participant can take is a side door: no
permission check was written for it, and whoever lacks it — an agent running
the table, a player on a phone — is a guest rather than a participant.

**Effect.** Any action a person can take from their window, an agent in the
same role can take with its tools, and the reverse, with the same result;
every tool that changes a game goes through the same interface.

### 2. The record of what happened only grows.

**Why.** Someone has already read what a rewrite would remove, so a record
that can be changed disagrees with the table's memory, and neither can be
trusted.

**Effect.** A mistake is corrected by a further entry, and the record shows
both the mistake and the correction; no operation removes or rewrites an
entry.

### 3. Every value at the table is explained by what happened.

**Why.** A value that nothing in the record explains cannot be audited,
disputed, replayed or caught up on: "why does my character have 12 left?" has
no answer.

**Effect.** Replaying a campaign's record reproduces its present exactly,
dice included; every value in the present traces to the entries that produced
it.

### 4. Every entry is judged in one place, against the whole record before it.

**Why.** An entry judged against a partial or stale view can contradict one
already accepted, and a record that contradicts itself no longer opens.

**Effect.** Two conflicting entries are never both accepted, whoever sent them
and however many copies of the system are pointed at one campaign; every
campaign's record opens.

### 5. What the system has once accepted, it accepts for ever.

**Why.** A record is kept for ever, so a check tightened later turns
yesterday's campaign into one that no longer opens, and an interface changed
later turns yesterday's window into one that can no longer speak.

**Effect.** Every record written by an earlier version of the system opens in
every later one; an operation, once offered, is never withdrawn and never
changes meaning.

### 6. Every decision is made where the record is kept.

**Why.** Whatever a participant runs, that participant can change; a rule
decided there, or a secret hidden there, is decided and hidden by them.

**Effect.** A participant's window or tool, however altered, can do nothing an
unaltered one cannot, and is sent nothing an unaltered one is not.

### 7. What a participant may do is judged each time they do it.

**Why.** A permission remembered from the moment someone connected keeps a
removed person at the table and keeps a promoted one out of what they were
given.

**Effect.** A change of role, of control or of admission applies to the
participant's very next action and to the very next thing they are sent, with
no reconnect.

### 8. Who may sit at the table is kept apart from what happened at it.

**Why.** Admission kept in the game's record comes back with every replay:
doors reopened, removed participants restored, credentials issued again.

**Effect.** Replaying or copying a campaign's record admits nobody and
restores no removed participant.

### 9. A request is carried out whole or not at all.

**Why.** A request half carried out is an action nobody asked for, and a
refusal that leaves a trace is a lever anyone outside the table can pull.

**Effect.** After a refusal the record and the present are exactly as they
were, and a refused stranger costs the table nothing; no request is answered
as done while any part of it was dropped.

### 10. Whoever perceives an act can tell who did it.

**Why.** A table where one participant can act or speak in another's name, or
where two answer to one name, cannot settle who did something, and neither can
its record.

**Effect.** Every entry names the participant who caused it; whoever is sent
an act is sent who did it, as their character knows them; nobody can cause an
entry in another's name or speak as a character they do not play; no two
participants at one table share a name.

### 11. Every reader of the record derives the same thing from it.

**Why.** A window that reads the record differently from where it is kept
shows a game that is not the one being played.

**Effect.** An entry refused where the record is kept is refused by every
reader, and every reader derives the same present from the same entries.

### 12. Nothing changes in a participant's place at the table without their being told.

**Why.** A participant who loses a character, a connection or a request
without a word does not know what they can still do, and acts on a table that
is no longer there.

**Effect.** A lost connection, a refused request, a character taken back and
a change of role are each shown to the participant they affect, when they
happen.

### 13. What a participant is told is said in the table's words.

**Why.** A message that names the system's insides tells a player nothing they
can act on, and shows them parts of the system that are not the game.

**Effect.** No message a participant receives names an internal part of the
system, shows an identifier where a name exists, or calls an entry by its
internal type.

### 14. Looking never acts.

**Why.** The record only grows, so an action taken by accident stays in it for
good.

**Effect.** Selecting, inspecting or pointing at anything adds nothing to the
record; every entry comes from something a participant meant as an act.

### 15. A new map or picture is used without stopping the table.

**Why.** A table that must stop to take a new map or picture stops in the
middle of a session, for everyone at it.

**Effect.** A map or a piece of art installed while a campaign is being played
can be brought into it at once, with nobody disconnected and nothing
restarted.

## The board engine

### 16. Everyone but the DM experiences the world through one character: a player with characters of their own, through the one of theirs they have chosen.

**Why.** A player sent the world sees what their character cannot, and the
table's secrets then rest on the player's restraint.

**Effect.** A player is sent exactly what the character they control and
currently follow perceived, and what the table was told, nothing more and
nothing less; they cannot follow a character they do not control, and when
control of it ends, so does their view through it. A spectator, and a player
who controls no character, follows a party member of their choosing in the
same way. Only the DM is sent the world.

### 17. Beyond what a character perceives, only the DM's story reaches its player.

**Why.** An engine that decides what a character would notice of what it
cannot perceive is a rule written into the board, the same for every game and
every scene; only the DM knows what the table needs to hear of it.

**Effect.** Of an act outside a character's perception its player is sent
nothing as data, not even that it happened; what the act does to what the
character does perceive, its own wounds among them, reaches the player without
its cause. Anything more reaches them only as the DM's telling — Lera cries
out as something slams into her with great force — and it reaches them as
story, never as an outcome of the rules.

### 18. What a character perceived is settled when it happens.

**Why.** A character's memory rewritten by what happens later tells the
player what the character never saw, or takes back what it did.

**Effect.** Each entry in a character's record is decided against the world as
it stood at that moment, and once written it never changes.

### 19. When it cannot be told whether something may be shown, it is not shown.

**Why.** A guess that shows is a leak no later entry can take back; a guess
that withholds costs a moment of play.

**Effect.** An entry the system cannot classify for a character is left out
of that character's record; nothing unrecognised reaches a player or a
spectator.

### 20. A player can act only on what their character perceives.

**Why.** An action aimed at something unseen tells the player it exists, and
lets a character act on what it cannot know.

**Effect.** A player's action aimed at, or moving into, what their character
does not perceive is answered exactly as if that thing did not exist.

### 21. Every measure on the board comes from the ruleset.

**Why.** A distance built into the board is one game's distance, and every
other game inherits it.

**Effect.** Two rulesets with different sight ranges, speeds or reaches run on
one board engine, unchanged, and their characters see, move and reach
differently.

### 22. A participant's place at the table survives losing the connection.

**Why.** A connection drops for reasons nobody at the table chose; a seat lost
with it punishes the participant for the network.

**Effect.** A participant who comes back controls the same characters and
looks through the same eyes as when they left; control ends only when someone
with the authority ends it.

## The rules engine

### 23. A game system exists only in its ruleset.

**Why.** A game system written into the engine is a fork: the agent cannot
read it, the DM cannot look it up, and every other system pays for it.

**Effect.** Installing a new game system changes nothing in the engine; no
game system's words appear in it; two unrelated game systems run on the same
engine unchanged.

### 24. The engine runs only the forms its contract defines.

**Why.** An engine that runs whatever a ruleset says is an embedded scripting
language, and brings back what one costs: rules nobody can read, state nobody
can see, limits discovered in play.

**Effect.** No ruleset can loop, keep variables or call anything it has not
declared; a new kind of mechanic arrives as a change to the engine, never
inside a ruleset.

### 25. A ruleset is checked whole before it is used.

**Why.** An error found at the table stops play at the moment it matters, in
front of everyone.

**Effect.** A ruleset with a form the contract does not define, a dangling
reference, an expression that does not parse or a value out of bounds is
refused when it is loaded, with the fault named; nothing about a ruleset's own
consistency is discovered during play.

### 26. Campaign data fits the ruleset it is played under.

**Why.** A sheet that does not fit its ruleset fails at its first roll, at the
table, and the failure looks like the rules' fault.

**Effect.** A character, creature or item that does not fit the campaign's
ruleset is refused when it enters the campaign, whichever way it enters. A
campaign is loaded with one ruleset; one whose data does not fit the ruleset it
is loaded with is refused, with what does not fit named, and bringing it up to
date is the DM's.

### 27. A sheet refers to its ruleset; it never copies from it.

**Why.** A copied weapon drifts from the list it came from: a correction
reaches only the sheets nobody had copied yet, and the table plays two
versions of one rule.

**Effect.** A sheet names the ruleset's entries it uses — a weapon, an armour,
an ability — and a correction to an entry reaches every sheet that names it;
a character is offered only what its sheet names.

### 28. Every outcome names the rules that decided it.

**Why.** Rules are corrected between sessions; an outcome that does not say
which rules produced it cannot be explained or disputed afterwards.

**Effect.** For any outcome in the record, the ruleset and the version of it
that decided it can be named; a correction to a ruleset shows in the record as
the point from which outcomes name the new version.

### 29. Every outcome records how it was reached.

**Why.** An outcome the table cannot follow is one it cannot trust or dispute,
and one the agent cannot explain.

**Effect.** The record of any outcome holds each die and its value, each
contribution and where it came from, and what the total was compared against;
which parts each participant is sent is decided as for any other entry.

### 30. Whoever rolls a die, the rules treat its value alike.

**Why.** A table that rolls its own dice plays the same game as one that lets
the platform roll, and a platform that allows only one way turns the other
table away.

**Effect.** A value a participant enters is resolved exactly as one the
platform rolled; a value the die cannot show is refused; the record says which
of the two it was.

### 31. The engine owns no unit of time.

**Why.** A turn built into the engine makes a game that counts ticks, or
seconds, impossible to run on it.

**Effect.** A ruleset that counts rounds and one that counts ticks run on the
same engine; every duration ends on its ruleset's own clock, which the engine
only advances.

### 32. A ruleset's story is offered to the DM, never imposed on the table.

**Why.** The same critical hit told as the book wrote it and as the DM tells
it are different stories, and only the DM knows which one the table needs.

**Effect.** A ruleset's descriptive text for an outcome reaches the table only
when the DM uses it; the outcome's mechanics apply either way.

## The interfaces: campaign and rules

### 33. The interface has one description.

**Why.** Two descriptions of one interface drift apart, and each participant's
software trusts its own.

**Effect.** Every window, every agent tool and the place the record is kept
take their operations from one description; an operation changed there changes
for all of them at once.

### 34. What the system says about itself is true.

**Why.** An agent acts on the descriptions it is given as a player acts on the
rules they were told; a false description makes the agent wrong with full
confidence, and nothing at the table shows it.

**Effect.** Following an operation's description never yields an outcome the
description rules out: what it says is refused is refused, and what it says is
shown is shown.

### 35. Every rule the engine can run, a participant can read.

**Why.** A rule the agent cannot read is one it cannot explain to the table or
run as DM; a player who cannot read what their character can do plays blind.

**Effect.** Every ability, item, condition and table in a ruleset can be
fetched through the interface as data, with the ruleset's own explanation, by
whoever may see it.
